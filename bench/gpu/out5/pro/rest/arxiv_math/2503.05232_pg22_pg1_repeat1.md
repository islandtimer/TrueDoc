where the first inequalities are ensured by Theorem 2. However, the irreducibility condition $(H\kappa_{irr})$ is not satisfied by $\kappa^{red}$ , so uniqueness is not ensured and Proposition 1 does not apply to guarantee that $N_1^{red} and N_2^{red}$ are non-zero. Adapting the proof of Proposition 1 to each of the strongly connected components of the graph of $\kappa$ , simply reduced to $\{v_1\} and \{v_2\}$ , shows that for each $i \in \{1, 2\} , N_i^{red}$ is either zero everywhere or positive on $(\frac{b}{2}, +\infty)$ . The normalization condition

$$\sum_{i \in \{1, 2\}} \int_0^\infty N_i^{red}(x) dx = 1$$

implies that at least for one $i \in \{1, 2\} , N_i^{red}$ is non-zero. Both $N_1^{red} and N_2^{red}$ cannot be non-zero, otherwise Lemma 2 would work (in equality (14), we need the existence of $i \neq j$ such that $N_i^{red} \neq 0 and N_j^{red} \neq 0$ ) and we could use it to prove the long-time convergence of system (20) towards a stationary state (fourth step of Theorem 4). That would contradict [6]. The only possibility is that $N_1^{red} \equiv 0 and N_2^{red}(x) > 0$ for $x \geq \frac{b}{2}$ or the opposite and then $\lambda = \lambda_2$ or $\lambda = \lambda_1$ respectively.

In the mixing case, the equations of system $(GF_{t,v})$ are coupled through their source term:

$$\begin{cases} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} n_1(t, x) + v_1 \frac{\partial}{\partial x} (x n_1(t, x)) + \gamma_1(x) n_1(t, x) = 4\gamma_2(2x) n_2(t, 2x), \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial t} n_2(t, x) + v_2 \frac{\partial}{\partial x} (x n_2(t, x)) + \gamma_2(x) n_2(t, x) = 4\gamma_1(2x) n_1(t, 2x), \\ n_1(0, x) = n_1^{in}(x), \quad n_2(0, x) = n_2^{in}(x), \end{cases}$$

and the irreducibility condition $(H\kappa_{irr})$ , missing in the non-mixing case, is now satisfied. We can thus apply successively Theorem 3, Proposition 1 and Theorem 4 to get eigenelements $(\lambda^{irr}, N^{irr}, \phi^{irr})$ such that

$$\sum_{i=1}^2 \int_{\mathbb{R}^+} |n_i(t, x) e^{-\lambda^{irr} t} - N_i^{irr}(x)| \phi_i^{irr}(x) dx \xrightarrow{t \rightarrow \infty} 0$$

with $\lambda^{irr} > 0$ , and for $i \in \{1, 2\} , N_i^{irr}$ positive on $(\frac{b}{2}, +\infty) and \phi_i^{irr} > 0$ on $(0, +\infty)$ .

These two simple cases illustrate that the existence result (Theorem 3) holds for every probability matrix $\kappa$ , in particular reducible ones. The irreducibility condition on $\kappa$ comes into play to characterize the functions canceling the dissipation of entropy (Lemma 2), which then proves crucial to establish uniqueness of the steady state and convergence towards it.
