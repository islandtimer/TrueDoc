2.3. Markov Dynamics. If $(X, \mathcal{B})$ is a measurable space and $U$ is a topological space, a stochastic kernel $P$ from $U$ to $X$ is a function $P: U \times \mathcal{B} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}_+$ with the property that $u \mapsto P(u, B)$ is Borel measurable for each $B \in \mathcal{B} and B \mapsto P(u, B)$ is a probability on $(X, \mathcal{B})$ for all $u \in U$ . When $U = X$ , we say that $P$ is a stochastic kernel on $X$ . Given a stochastic kernel $P$ from $U$ to $X$ , we set

$$(Ph)(u) := \int h(x')P(u, dx') \quad (h \in bmX, u \in U).$$

When $X$ is a topological space we use the following standard terminology: We call $P$ Feller if $Ph$ is continuous on $U$ whenever $h \in bmX$ is continuous on $X$ . We call $P$ strong Feller if $Ph$ is continuous on $U$ whenever $h \in bmX$ .

3. ABSTRACT DYNAMIC PROGRAMS

This section is a brief review of the dynamic programming framework from Sargent and Stachurski (2025) . We repeat the key definitions to make the paper partly self-contained.

3.1. Definition and Properties. An abstract dynamic program (ADP) is a pair $(V, \mathbb{T})$ , where

(i) $V = (V, \preceq)$ is a poset and (ii) $\mathbb{T} = \{T_\sigma : \sigma \in \Sigma\}$ is a nonempty family of order preserving self-maps on $V$ .

We call $V$ the value space . Each operator $T_\sigma$ in $\mathbb{T}$ is called a policy operator . $\Sigma$ is an arbitrary index set and elements of $\Sigma$ are called policies . In applications, we impose conditions under which each $T_\sigma$ has a unique fixed point. In these settings, the significance of each policy operator $T_\sigma$ is that its fixed point, denoted below by $v_\sigma$ , represents the lifetime value of following policy $\sigma$ .

Let $(V, \mathbb{T})$ be an ADP with policy set $\Sigma$ . Given $v \in V$ , a policy $\sigma \in \Sigma$ is called $v$ -greedy if $T_\tau v \preceq T_\sigma v$ for all $\tau \in \Sigma$ . An element $v \in V$ is said to satisfy the Bellman equation if

$$v := \bigvee_{\sigma} T_\sigma v \quad (v \in V). \quad (1)$$

In (1), the supremum is taken over all $\sigma \in \Sigma$ . The Bellman operator generated by $(V, \mathbb{T})$ is defined via

$$Tv := \bigvee_{\sigma} T_\sigma v \quad \text{whenever the supremum exists.}$$
